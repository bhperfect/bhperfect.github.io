Route

“Dragontail tomorrow?” Nick asked. “I’m going to a bday party tomorrow, but how about Sunday?”

We agreed to try for Backbone Ridge, a committing 5.9 route, but noted that Serpentine Arete, a 5.8 of similar length, but slightly lower alpine grade started from the same location. We departed Seattle at 4am. Anticipating a long day, I made sure to toss a few Red Bulls into my bag of car goodies, which proved to be an excellent move. I considered crampons, but I have semi-auto crampons, and I wanted to avoid bringing along bulky mountaineering boots. I brought an ice axe to attempt to make up for this deficit, and chose to bring Jasmine’s mostly for the weight savings. Afterwards, I realize that the sharper pick on her newer axe probably saved me a good deal of discomfort, if not serious injury.

We made the drive up to Stuart Lake Trailhead and crushed the familiar trail up to and around Colchuck Lake in great time, perhaps 2 hours. After chatting briefly with some thru-hikers, we diverged from the trail and headed up towards the Colchuck Glacier. Disappointingly, the start for Backbone was guarded by a snowfield that turned out to be quite icy. We were marginal on time, and not quite sure where the route started. The annoyance of an icy traverse without crampons pushed me over the edge to vote on doing Serpentine, which in retrospect was absolutely the right decision. We scrambled some 3rd-4th class until the difficulties demanded protection. I took the first pitch and quickly ended up with a tricky part of the pitch that required some downclimbing and I sewed up with gear. Rope drag limited my climbing to about 30m and I belayed Nick up from a tree with a loose alpine draw on it. I bootied the draw and Nick led up a similarly tricky section. We simuled for a bit, doing perhaps 270 feet. I then led up an interesting corner system that was supposed to take us to the crux, but again rope drag became a problem, and I only managed 40 meters or so. Nick’s next pitch included some heinous off width that I fell on 4 times before finally making it up. Nick also led the crux pitch, linking together two 5.8 sections, the first of which was a finger crack that I gleefully French freed, and the second of which was a fun hand crack. I think Nick did one more pitch before the main difficulties were over. I then led 3 simul-climbing pitches of decent length, ending a short bit before the summit, just as cloud cover moved in and light snow began.

We didn’t take very long on the summit, and scrambled down the standard route, which I had done 3 years previously. This late in the season, things looked quite different; the snowfield that I had traversed down to Aasgard pass back in May was now solid ice, starting at the bottom of a 15 foot cliff. Nick wanted to scout for an easier route, but I knew the truth; we would have to rappel or downclimb, and then deal with the snow. Visibility was poor enough that we could not see the runout. After scouting and confirming my suspicions, we downclimbed for a bit and then jumped the moat to get onto the ice. Testing my footing on the flat top of the moat, I knew we would be in for a struggle. Nick in crampons, but with no axe, and myself with an axe but not crampons, we started the traverse.

In retrospect, we should have given both pieces of gear to one person and then allowed the other to do single rope rappel/hand lining until getting off of the steep section. Except once reaching the bottom of the rope, the person would have no way to dig in… Maybe there was no good way to do this without an ice screw.

For the traverse, I had Nick lead and try to kick tiny edges for me to use for purchase. The loose snow he scraped out also helped with the friction. 15 steps in, we were moving slowly, but making progress. I was using my axe in self-belay mode, but the inability to penetrate the ice meant that it wasn’t doing much besides acting as support. Suddenly, my feet slipped out from under me, and I started sliding down the slope. I’m amazed I had the presence of mind to call out, “falling!” as I felt my feet blow out. I gained speed quickly, but my training kicked in, and I dug in the pick. I managed to control the slide, and eventually stop myself. Unable to kick steps or stand on the slope, I had to stay in self arrest position until Nick was able to carefully downclimb to me and kick some steps. I was by far the less stable one, but Nick would have no means of self arresting if he slipped. He was strictly in no-fall zone. Realizing we were temping fate, we traversed as efficiently as possible over to a rock band. I switched over to high dagger mode and began supporting my weight with the axe. Even though the axe was only penetrating 5-10mm into the ice, it was enough to prevent any more falls.

Before starting the traverse, I had opted to attach my axe to my harness with an alpine draw, which proved to be an extremely important move. Two days after this traverse, my left arm would be incredible sore, which I realized was because of how much effort I was exerting holding myself up using the axe. For around an hour, I was holding that axe like it was my lifeline.

We made it to the rock, which was actually incredibly loose rock/dirt and traversed for a bit. Nick crossed some even more bulletproof ice to get to some nicer rock, but there was no way I would be able to follow him. I tossed an end of the rope across the 50 foot gap and he attached his crampons, which I hauled back over to make the traverse. I scrambled over to Nick, who had found a decent cave. It was only 7:45 at this point, but it was pitch black and snowing, with limited visibility. It was time to have an earnest chat about our situation. As the crow flies, we were not far from friendly terrain, but the route to get there was unknown. We considered hunkering down for the night, but it was still relatively early, and we felt we could potentially scramble down on rock. We scrambled generally down and left, trending towards the mellow bottom of the bowl we were above. Whenever we got cliffed out, we were able to find an alternate route. Finally, we were faced with an insurmountable cliff. Unable to see more than 15 feet, there was no telling how big the cliff was, though the topo map indicated we were in a relatively flat area. There was no chance of a comfortable bivy in our location, so we slung a rock and rappelled into the void. 20 feet down, across a bergschrund, and we were on mellow snow. I left my quad cordalette and a locking biner that I had bootied earlier in the day, but I was happy to trade them for familiar terrain.

We blindly wandered in the pitch black until we met up with the Aasgard Pass trail around 9:30pm, and then followed that down. I took great pains to not lose the trail, but in a section where Nick was leading, the trail seemed to get progressively fainter. I was too tired to question his routefinding, but it turned out that Nick was too tired to care about the route. I finally said something as we entered thick brush. Not only were we off route, we had been off route for long enough that backtracking to look for the trail was a lost cause. During the miserable bushwhack that ensued, I managed to take a few falls and inflict incalculable cuts and bruises. By the end, I had abandoned all pretense of rejoining the trail, and made a grunting, staggering beeline for the shore of Colchuck lake. Near midnight, we finally emerged on the lake shore, and sat. My injuries from my falls early were aching, and my semi-healed achilles and knee from my century the last week were acting up in a big way. We discussed sleeping then and there, but decided that in our relatively exposed position we would quickly get cold. And besides, it was “only” a 5 mile hike back to the car.

1.5 miles later, we were delirious, out of water, and exhausted. Nick fell behind, and I waited for a few minutes, asking how he was doing. He declared he needed a nap, so we found a flat area and huddled under a space blanket and sleeping bag in the rain. There wasn’t really anywhere that was both a good place to sleep and sheltered, so we chose a flat spot next to a boulder and laid down. We slept for perhaps 40 minutes, and then regrouped and hiked the rest of the way out. We reached the cars around 3:45am, downed some energy drinks, and drove home in shifts. I got home at 6:20am, making for a nearly 27-hour day.
